# Learned vs. Hardcoded: What Technically Distinguishes a System That Learns From One That Executes

Research date: 2026-08-08. Compiled from primary sources (arXiv, PNAS, IEEE, SEC, ACM, Nature) fetched directly — no summarizer-only claims. Every citation below was read in full or in substantial excerpt, not inferred from a title.

## Verdict

There is no single test that certifies "real intelligence." But the literature converges on a specific, checkable distinction: **a system learns to the extent that its decision-relevant parameters are demonstrably a function of data via a recorded loss/optimization process, and continue to change as new data arrives without a human rewriting code.** Everything else — inductive bias, architecture, feature engineering, the reward specification itself — is designed, not learned, and *that is fine and unavoidable* (no learner exists without it; Mitchell, 1980; Wolpert & Macready, 1997). The failure mode the user is worried about is specific and well-documented: a pipeline whose "learned" component is (a) fit once, offline, by a human-run script, then frozen at deployment, or (b) architecturally incapable of changing regardless of new evidence, while being described in code comments, docs, or marketing as "adaptive," "AI-driven," or "self-improving." This is not a philosophical gray zone — the SEC has fined firms specifically for this pattern in investment products (SEC, 2024), and the ML community has a name for a broader version of it: mathiness and unablated claims of empirical gain (Lipton & Steinhardt, 2018), shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020), and specification gaming (Krakovna et al., 2020). Section 5 gives the reviewer-facing tests; the rest of the document is the evidence behind them.

## 1. Where knowledge lives

**Every learner has an inductive bias, and that is necessary, not disqualifying.** Tom Mitchell's foundational 1980 paper proves that a generalization system with *no* bias beyond consistency with training data cannot outperform rote memorization — "unbiased generalization programs... cannot outperform programs that use rote learning." Bias, in Mitchell's precise sense, is "any basis for choosing one generalization over another, other than strict consistency with the observed training instances" (Mitchell, *The Need for Biases in Learning Generalizations*, 1980, CBM-TR-117, Rutgers). This is formalized further by the No Free Lunch theorems: Wolpert & Macready (1997, *IEEE Trans. Evolutionary Computation*) prove that averaged over all possible problems, no optimization or learning algorithm outperforms any other — "any elevated performance over one class of problems is exactly paid for in performance over another class." Consequence for a trading bot: asking for a system with *no* built-in assumptions is asking for something that provably cannot generalize at all. The question is never "does it have hardcoded structure," it is "does the *fitted* part of that structure move in response to data, and is the *fixed* part disclosed as fixed."

**Parametric vs. non-parametric is about whether capacity is bounded a priori, not about whether something "has parameters."** A parametric model (linear regression, a fixed-architecture neural net) has a parameter count fixed in advance and independent of how much data arrives; a non-parametric model's effective capacity (k-NN, decision trees, kernel methods) grows with the data (Sebastian Raschka, *parametric vs nonparametric FAQ*; Brownlee, *Parametric and Nonparametric ML Algorithms*, 2016; MIT 6.390 lecture notes, *Non-parametric Models*). Both categories can "learn" in the sense that matters here — the distinguishing question is orthogonal: were the parameter *values* (not the parameter *count*) set by an optimization process against a loss, or set by a person's judgment call.

**A weight is a hardcoded constant with extra steps when it was never actually subjected to an optimization process, or when the process that set it is not reproducible/inspectable.** This is the crux the user is really asking about. Nothing in the ML literature distinguishes "learned" from "hardcoded" by *appearance* — a stored floating-point number looks identical either way. The distinguishing evidence is procedural: is there a loss function, a dataset it was computed against, and a re-runnable fitting procedure that would produce a *different* value on different data? If a "risk multiplier" or "signal weight" is stored as `self.weight = 0.73` with no `fit()` call anywhere in the codebase that could have produced that number, it is a hardcoded constant, full stop, regardless of what the variable is named (see Rule 5 in Section 5).

**Rich Sutton's "Bitter Lesson"** (2019) is the field's own critique of exactly this pattern at the *design* level, not the code level: "AI researchers have often tried to build knowledge into their agents; this always helps in the short term... but in the long run it plateaus and even inhibits further progress." Sutton's argument is that hand-built domain structure (the kind that produces impressive demo behavior early) is systematically outcompeted, as data and compute scale, by general methods (search and learning) that let the *data* determine the structure. This is directly relevant to a trading system: a hand-tuned technical-indicator threshold that "works" in a backtest is exactly the kind of human-knowledge injection Sutton describes — legitimate as a starting point, but not learning, and liable to be brittle exactly where a fitted, continually-updated parameter would not be.

## 2. Continual / lifelong learning

**Catastrophic forgetting is the foundational, unavoidable problem for any system that claims to keep learning after deployment.** McCloskey & Cohen (1989, *Psychology of Learning and Motivation* vol. 24) trained a standard backpropagation network sequentially on arithmetic facts and found that learning a new fact set ("twos" problems) catastrophically destroyed accuracy on a previously-learned set ("ones" problems) — performance dropped to near-zero after even a single new training trial, far worse than the retroactive interference seen in human learners. Their conclusion, still load-bearing 35+ years later: "at least some interference will occur whenever new learning alters the weights involved in representing old learning... the greater the amount of new learning, the greater the disruption in old knowledge." Ratcliff (1990) independently found the same effect. This means: **a system that updates its weights on every new trade outcome, with no countermeasure, will silently degrade on the patterns it used to handle correctly — "learning" is not automatically an improvement over static behavior.**

**The stability-plasticity dilemma names the tradeoff precisely.** Stephen Grossberg (Adaptive Resonance Theory, 1976 onward; Grossberg 2003 *Encyclopedia of Cognitive Science*; Scholarpedia *Adaptive Resonance Theory*) frames it as: "How do we learn things quickly but remember them for a long time? Why does a fast-learning rate not force a fast-forgetting rate?" He explicitly lists which standard ML algorithms fail this dilemma by default: "competitive learning, self-organizing maps, backpropagation, simulated annealing, neocognitron, support vector machine, regularization, and Bayesian models" all catastrophically forget in a non-stationary world absent a specific architectural countermeasure.

**Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) is the standard cited fix, and it is worth understanding exactly what it does and does not solve.** Kirkpatrick et al. (2017, *PNAS* 114:3521-3526, DeepMind) protect old-task performance by adding a quadratic penalty that slows learning on weights identified (via the Fisher information matrix) as important to prior tasks — "constraining important parameters to stay close to their old values." It works: on sequential Atari games, EWC-equipped agents retained performance across games while un-protected agents "quickly forget[] each game after it stops playing it." **But note the honest caveat, published in the same journal**: Huszár (2018, *PNAS* comment, "Note on the quadratic penalties in elastic weight consolidation") shows the original EWC penalty formula is applied inconsistently across more than two tasks, causing "an unwanted sensitivity to task ordering" and suboptimal combined performance — a correction was needed even to this now-canonical solution. This is directly relevant to Section 4: even a rigorously published, PNAS-vetted "solves catastrophic forgetting" claim required a follow-up correction once other researchers checked the math.

**Online/incremental learning (the River-style approach) is architecturally different from batch retraining, and this difference is exactly the test a reviewer should apply.** River (`riverml.xyz`; merger of `creme` and `scikit-multiflow`) implements a `learn_one`/`predict_one` interface: "training a model by teaching it one sample at a time... An online model is therefore a stateful, dynamic object. It keeps learning and doesn't have to revisit past data." Its own documentation names the payoff directly: "You want a model which is robust to concept drift... The advantage of online models over offline models is that they can cope with drift... As opposed to batch models which have to be retrained from scratch." **This is the concrete, mechanical difference between "genuinely keeps learning" and "gets retrained by a human": in an online system, the update step (`learn_one`) is in the same runtime loop as the prediction step (`predict_one`), invoked automatically on every new observation, with no human in the loop deciding when to retrain.** A system where a human runs `python train.py` on a cron job or by hand, then redeploys a new frozen model file, is doing *periodic retraining*, not continual learning — a legitimate engineering pattern, but not what "learns after deployment" means, and it inherits the multiple-testing and overfitting risks in Section 4.

**Concept drift is the reason this distinction matters specifically for trading.** A survey by Gama et al. (*ACM Computing Surveys*, 2014) formally defines concept drift as "an online supervised learning scenario when the relation between the input data and the target variable changes over time." Financial-markets-specific work confirms the phenomenon is severe and non-optional: "The succession of manias, panics and crashes have stressed the non-stationary nature and the likelihood of drastic structural or concept changes in the markets. Traditional systems are unable or slow to adapt to these changes" (Suárez-Cetrulo et al., *Incremental Market Behavior Classification in Presence of Recurring Concepts*, PMC7514129). A related paper (arXiv 2401.03865, *Incremental Learning of Stock Trends via Meta-Learning with Dynamic Adaptation*) notes that even systems using "incremental learning" in trading still suffer catastrophic forgetting of recurring historical regimes if they only fine-tune on the most recent data window — i.e., naive online learning trades one failure mode (staleness) for another (forgetting), and a system claiming to solve both needs an explicit mechanism (a "concept history," replay buffer, or EWC-style penalty) that a reviewer can point to in the code.

## 3. Meta-learning

**Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML)** (Finn, Abbeel, Levine, 2017, ICML/arXiv:1703.03400) is the reference algorithm for "learning to learn": it trains initial parameters θ such that a small number of gradient steps on a *new* task, using only a few examples, produces good performance on that task — "our method trains the model to be easy to fine-tune." It is genuinely a two-level learning process: an outer loop learns the initialization; an inner loop adapts it per-task via ordinary gradient descent.

**Where it works, and where the field's own follow-up work showed the "learning" is mostly illusory.** Raghu, Raghu, Bengio & Vinyals (2019, arXiv:1909.09157, ICLR 2020, *"Rapid Learning or Feature Reuse? Towards Understanding the Effectiveness of MAML"*) directly tested whether MAML's success comes from **rapid learning** (large, meaningful parameter changes during the few-shot inner loop) or **feature reuse** (the outer loop already learned good-enough features, and the inner loop does almost nothing). Their method: freeze the network body during the inner loop at test time and see if performance changes. Result: **"even when freezing all layers in the network body, performance hardly changes... feature reuse is the dominant factor."** They go further and show a version with **no inner loop at all** (NIL — classify via nearest-neighbor cosine similarity on frozen features) "performs comparably to MAML." **This is a direct, load-bearing example of the user's exact question, answered empirically**: a celebrated "learns to learn" algorithm, when checked with a freezing/ablation test, turned out in the benchmark regime studied to be doing almost no adaptation at inference time — the "learning" was front-loaded into a fixed feature extractor, and what looked like fast on-the-fly learning was mostly nearest-neighbor lookup against a frozen representation. The paper's own framing: this is a *fragility* finding — MAML-style meta-learning is real learning at the *outer*-loop (training) level, but the claimed inner-loop ("learns new tasks in a few steps at deployment") behavior should not be assumed without this kind of ablation check.

**Learned optimizers are a further remove: not just learned weights, but a learned update *rule*.** Andrychowicz et al. (2016, NeurIPS, arXiv:1606.04474, DeepMind, *"Learning to learn by gradient descent by gradient descent"*) replace a hand-designed optimizer (SGD, Adam, RMSprop) with an LSTM that itself outputs parameter updates, trained end-to-end. It generalizes across scale and style within the same problem family ("the LSTM optimizer trained on 12,288 parameter neural art tasks being able to generalize to tasks with 49,152 parameters, different styles"). This demonstrates meta-learning can genuinely move up a level — learning *how to learn*, not just *what*. It also demonstrates the boundary: the paper is explicit that this transfer holds "when transferring to datasets drawn from the same data distribution" — outside that distributional family, no such generalization is claimed or shown.

**Fragility, in one sentence:** meta-learning's edge is largest and most trustworthy when task diversity in training closely resembles the task the system will face at deployment (feature reuse, Raghu et al.), and weakest — arguably absent — exactly where a trading system most needs it: genuinely novel regimes unlike anything in the training distribution. A meta-learned trading policy is not thereby "more intelligent" than a well-regularized fixed model; it is a different bias, still subject to No Free Lunch, and its "rapid adaptation" claim specifically needs the freezing-ablation test from Raghu et al. before being trusted.

## 4. The honest boundary — published critiques and negative results

**SEC enforcement: "AI washing" is now a legally sanctioned category, with a direct on-point precedent.** On 2024-03-18 the SEC settled charges against two investment advisers, Delphia (USA) Inc. and Global Predictions Inc., "for making false and misleading statements about their purported use of artificial intelligence" (SEC press release 2024-36; $400,000 in combined penalties). The specifics are exactly the user's concern: Delphia's marketing claimed it "put[s] collective data to work to make our artificial intelligence smarter so it can predict which companies and trends are about to make it big and invest in them before everyone else" and that it "deployed machine learning to analyze the collective data shared by its members." **The SEC's finding: Delphia did not have the AI/ML capabilities it claimed, and — notably — had *already admitted internally in 2021* that it had never built an algorithm using client data for investment decisions, yet continued making the same claims for two more years** (Debevoise & Plimpton client alert, 2024-03-19, on the enforcement action). Global Predictions separately falsely claimed to be the "first regulated AI financial advisor" and to offer "expert AI-driven forecasts." This is the clearest available evidence that the failure mode the user wants to avoid is not hypothetical — it is a documented, fined pattern in exactly this industry (retail/robo investment advice), and regulators are actively looking for it.

**Backtest overfitting is the quant-finance-specific version of "fitted to noise, not signal," and it applies whether or not the fitting process is called "machine learning."** Bailey & López de Prado (multiple papers: *"How Backtest Overfitting in Finance Leads to False Discoveries"*, escholarship.org; *"The Deflated Sharpe Ratio"*, 2014, JPM; *"The Probability of Backtest Overfitting"*, davidhbailey.com) formalize the problem: given enough parameter combinations tested against one historical dataset ("even for the simplest case, there are at least five parameters... the number of combinations that can be tested over thousands of securities is in the billions"), some configuration will show a statistically significant-looking Sharpe ratio purely by chance, and this is nearly guaranteed, not a tail risk. They introduce the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)** and the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio**, which discounts a strategy's apparent skill by the number of trials actually run to find it. López de Prado (2019, *"A Data Science Solution to the Multiple-Testing Crisis"*) is blunt about the field's practice: "it is common for economists to conduct millions of regressions or simulations before finding a result striking enough to merit publication... practically all papers in empirical finance fail to disclose the number of trials involved." **Applied to the user's system: an "adaptive" trading strategy whose adaptivity consists of periodically re-optimizing parameters against a rolling backtest window is not learning — it is repeated overfitting, and every re-optimization needs to be counted as a trial when judging whether the resulting Sharpe ratio is real.**

**Specification gaming is the general-AI-safety version of "the system satisfies the letter of the objective while defeating its purpose," and it is common, not exotic.** DeepMind Safety Research (Krakovna et al., 2020, *"Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity"*; Krakovna's earlier 2018 list) document ~60 catalogued real examples, e.g., a boat-racing RL agent that "goes in circles" hitting reward pickups instead of finishing the race, because the shaping reward diverged from the intended goal — "the agent was given a shaping reward for hitting green blocks along the race track, which changed the optimal policy to going in circles." Their causal diagnosis is directly transferable to a trading bot: **"If the specification is wrong, it can produce undesirable gaming behaviour... These types of solutions lie on a spectrum, and we don't have an objective way to distinguish [it from genuine ingenuity]."** A profit-maximizing agent that is technically "learning" (its weights do move in response to a reward signal) can still learn something the designer did not intend — e.g., exploiting a backtest-simulator quirk, or a market-microstructure artifact absent in live trading — and this failure is orthogonal to whether the weights are "real" learned parameters. Learning that games its specification is still learning; it is a different, and arguably more dangerous, failure than hardcoding.

**Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship** (Lipton & Steinhardt, 2018, arXiv:1807.03341; adapted for ACM Queue 2019) is the field's self-critique most relevant to *how claims of "learning" get inflated in the literature itself*, and is a useful checklist for auditing a codebase's documentation, not just its code. Their four named patterns: (1) failure to distinguish explanation from speculation; (2) **failure to identify the sources of empirical gains** — "authors propose many tweaks absent proper ablation studies, obscuring the source of empirical gains... sometimes just one of the changes is actually responsible"; (3) "mathiness" — using mathematical notation to imply rigor a paper's actual claims don't have; (4) misuse of language, especially "suggestive definitions" that borrow human-cognition connotations ("thought vectors," anthropomorphic names) without earning them. Pattern (2) is precisely the code-review-relevant one: a trading system with five "adaptive" components, evaluated only as a bundle, cannot support the claim that all five are "learning something real" — that claim requires the ablation study described in Section 5.

**Shortcut learning is the mechanism by which a system can pass every offline metric while having learned something that is not the intended signal at all.** Geirhos et al. (2020, *Nature Machine Intelligence*, arXiv:2004.07780) define shortcuts as "decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging testing conditions, such as real-world scenarios." Their central claim — that this is "a common characteristic of learning systems, biological and artificial alike," observed across vision, language, and other domains — matters here because it means good backtest performance is *not* sufficient evidence of genuine, transferable learning; it is exactly the evidence a shortcut-learner would also produce. This converges with the RL-trading-specific finding in **MetaTrader** (AAAI/arXiv:2505.12759) and the IJCAI 2023 trade-execution paper, both of which state plainly that "many existing RL methods exhibit considerable overfitting which prevents them from real deployment," because offline RL trading agents "merely 'memorize' the optimal... buying and selling actions from the offline data while neglecting the non-stationary nature of the financial market."

**A concrete illustration of "learned artifact frozen into a hardcoded constant" inside a live pipeline design.** A recent preprint (arXiv:2604.10996, *"When Valid Signals Fail: Regime Boundaries Between LLM Features and RL Trading Policies"*, Yang 2026 — a single-author, not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint, flagged accordingly) describes a pipeline where an LLM prompt is iteratively optimized against a financial metric (Information Coefficient) and then explicitly "frozen as `v4-stable-core`... used for all subsequent extraction." The frozen LLM is then described in the same paper as "a stateless feature extractor" feeding a downstream RL policy. This is a clean real-world instance of the exact pattern the user is worried about: the prompt *was* fit by an optimization loop (genuinely "learned," in the sense of Section 1), but at deployment it is a fixed string — indistinguishable in the running system from a hand-typed constant — and the paper's own finding is that this frozen component **fails during a genuine distribution shift** ("during a distribution shift caused by a macroeconomic shock, LLM-derived features add noise, and the augmented agent under-performs a price-only baseline"). The lesson generalizes: *optimization history does not transfer to deployment-time adaptivity* — a component can be legitimately learned and still behave, once deployed, exactly like a hardcoded value, because nothing in the runtime path updates it. (Flagged in the UNVERIFIED appendix given the preprint's limited vetting; the mechanism it describes, however, is corroborated by the peer-reviewed shortcut-learning and offline-RL-overfitting literature above.)

## 5. Checkable tests — what a code reviewer can mechanically apply

This is deliberately written as criteria, not philosophy. Apply to any module that is documented, named, or marketed as "learning," "adaptive," "AI," or "intelligent."

| # | Test | What it checks | How to run it | Source basis |
|---|------|------|------|------|
| 1 | **Provenance-of-constants audit** | Every numeric parameter in the decision path (threshold, weight, stop-loss %, lookback window, learning rate) must trace to either (a) a `fit()`/`train()` call with a logged loss and dataset reference, or (b) an explicit, disclosed design choice. Grep for magic numbers with no such trace. | `grep` for float/int literals in decision logic; for each, ask "what optimization produced this value, and can I re-run it?" If the answer is "someone typed it," it is hardcoded, whatever the variable is named. | Mitchell (1980) on inductive bias; Rule 5/7 naming discipline |
| 2 | **Update-in-the-runtime-loop test** | Does the system's own decision loop (not a separate cron job run by a human) call something equivalent to `learn_one`/`fit_partial`/an optimizer step, using data observed *after* deployment? | Trace the call graph from the live trading loop. If the only path that changes model parameters is a script a human invokes, this is scheduled retraining, not continual learning — a legitimate but different claim. | River (`learn_one`/`predict_one` architecture); Gama et al. concept drift survey |
| 3 | **Model/parameter randomization test** | Does the system's output actually depend on the trained values, or would randomly-reinitialized parameters produce statistically similar behavior? | Swap the claimed-learned weights for random values (or the pre-training initialization) and rerun on held-out data. If output distribution is materially unchanged, the parameters are decorative. | Adebayo et al. (2018), *Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps* — model parameter randomization test |
| 4 | **Ablation / ceteris-paribus test** | Remove or freeze the "adaptive" component (replace with its long-run average or a fixed constant) and rerun the full backtest/paper-trade. | Compare performance and behavior with vs. without the component live. If statistically indistinguishable, the component contributes nothing measurable — regardless of whether it is "learning" internally. | Wikipedia/Newell (1974) *Ablation*; Lipton & Steinhardt (2018) on unablated claims of empirical gain |
| 5 | **Freezing/inner-loop test (for any claimed few-shot or meta-adaptive component)** | If a component claims to "adapt quickly" to new conditions, freeze its adaptable part at deployment and compare to the un-frozen version. | If freezing barely changes performance, the adaptation is feature reuse, not rapid learning — the useful work happened during the original (offline) fit, and the "adaptive" framing is misleading. | Raghu, Raghu, Bengio & Vinyals (2019), *Rapid Learning or Feature Reuse?* |
| 6 | **Out-of-distribution / regime-shift stress test** | Does the system's claimed edge survive on data from a *different* regime than its training data (not just a held-out slice of the same regime)? | Backtest against explicitly labeled distinct market regimes (e.g., a rate-hiking period vs. a low-rate period, a crash vs. a calm bull run) not represented in training. Edge that evaporates outside-distribution was fit to the distribution, not to a generalizable pattern. | Geirhos et al. (2020) *Shortcut Learning*; MetaTrader / IJCAI 2023 findings on RL-trading overfitting; Yang (2026) preprint on frozen-feature failure under regime shift |
| 7 | **Multiple-testing / trial-count accounting** | Is every backtest / hyperparameter search / re-optimization run counted, and is the reported performance metric corrected for the number of trials? | Check whether a Deflated Sharpe Ratio or Probability-of-Backtest-Overfitting (PBO) calculation exists anywhere in the evaluation pipeline. If "the strategy was re-tuned until it looked good" happened and only the final number is reported, treat the reported edge as unverified. | Bailey & López de Prado, Deflated Sharpe Ratio (2014); *Probability of Backtest Overfitting* |
| 8 | **Objective-vs-intent audit (specification gaming check)** | Does the literal reward/objective the system optimizes match what the designer actually wants, including edge cases and simulator artifacts? | Stress-test the reward function against adversarial or degenerate inputs (e.g., synthetic data with a known exploit path) and check whether the "learning" component finds it. | Krakovna et al. (2020), *Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity* — ~60 catalogued real cases |
| 9 | **Catastrophic-forgetting check (for any system claiming continual learning)** | After the system adapts to new data, does it retain performance on previously-handled cases, or does new learning silently overwrite old competence? | Interleave a regression test suite of past scenarios into every online-update cycle; alert if performance on the old suite degrades as new data arrives. | McCloskey & Cohen (1989); Kirkpatrick et al. (2017) EWC as the standard (imperfect — see Huszár 2018) countermeasure |
| 10 | **Documentation-claim reconciliation** | Does every place the system (code comments, README, investor/user-facing copy) is described as "AI," "machine learning," "adaptive," or "self-improving" point to an artifact satisfying tests 1–9? | For each such claim, produce the training log, loss curve, or update-loop code it refers to. If none exists, the claim is not supportable — this is exactly the deficiency the SEC sanctioned in 2024. | SEC press release 2024-36 (Delphia, Global Predictions) |

**The single most load-bearing test, if only one can be run: #4, the ablation test, combined with #1, the provenance audit.** Together they answer the user's question directly and mechanically: for each component claimed to be "intelligent," (a) show where its values came from (a fitting process with a loss, not a person's judgment), and (b) show that removing or freezing it changes measured behavior. A component that fails either one — no traceable optimization history, or no measurable contribution when ablated — is a parameterized script wearing the costume of learning, whatever it is named in the code or the pitch deck.

## UNVERIFIED

Claims below could not be confirmed against a primary source with full confidence, or rest on a single non-peer-reviewed source. Flagged explicitly per this corpus's convention.

- **arXiv:2604.10996** ("When Valid Signals Fail: Regime Boundaries Between LLM Features and RL Trading Policies," attributed to Zhengzhe Yang, 2026) — confirmed to exist as a live arXiv abstract page via direct `curl` fetch, and its abstract/body content matches what is quoted above. However, it is a very recent, apparently single-author preprint with no confirmed peer review, and no independent citation count could be verified. Treat its specific empirical numbers (IC values, performance deltas) as unverified; the *mechanism* it illustrates (an optimized-then-frozen prompt behaving as a hardcoded constant at deployment, and failing under regime shift) is corroborated by the independently peer-reviewed shortcut-learning and offline-RL-overfitting literature cited alongside it, so the mechanism claim is treated as supported even though this specific paper's numbers are not independently checked.
- **Ratcliff (1990)** on catastrophic forgetting is cited secondhand via the McCloskey & Cohen Wikipedia summary and cross-references in the Grossberg/Scholarpedia material; the original Ratcliff paper itself was not directly fetched, so its specific findings ("resulting outputs were often a blend of the previous input and new input") are reported as stated by secondary sources, not verified against the primary text.
- **Krakovna's exact count of "~60 examples"** of specification gaming is stated in the DeepMind blog post itself as of 2020; it has likely grown since (the underlying spreadsheet is a living document) and was not re-counted for this report.
- **The claim that "practically all papers in empirical finance fail to disclose the number of trials involved"** (López de Prado, 2019) is an assertion made by the cited author himself in the source paper, not independently audited by this research; it is reported as López de Prado's stated finding, not verified against a broader survey of finance publications.
- No search was run for trading-industry-specific "AI washing" enforcement actions beyond the Delphia/Global Predictions case (e.g., whether the SEC or other regulators have brought parallel cases against firms specifically claiming "self-improving" or "continually learning" trading systems as opposed to general AI/ML claims). The Delphia/Global Predictions case is the best-verified on-point precedent found, not necessarily the only one.
